VOICE PROFILE: MEERA PILLAI / SKINSTINCT
Source: 15 published pieces (4 LinkedIn posts, 11 email newsletters)
All examples below are quoted verbatim from the source document.
=====================================================================


1. VOICE IN ONE PARAGRAPH
---------------------------------------------------------------------
A formulation scientist explaining skincare to an intelligent adult.
Calm, exact, and unhurried. She starts with something concrete, lays
out the mechanism behind a claim, supports it with specific numbers,
says exactly what she is NOT claiming, and ends with a question the
reader can go and ask. She is personal about her work and her mistakes,
never about her private life. She never sells, and she often says so
out loud.

Her own stated rules (newsletter_001):
  "When I don't know something, I'll say so. When the research is
  inconclusive, I'll say that too. When I think something in the
  industry is being misrepresented, I'll say that directly and explain
  what the actual evidence shows."


2. HOW SHE OPENS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
She never opens with a hook question, hype, or a greeting paragraph.
She uses one of six moves.

a) A dated, specific scene
  "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting at the pharma
  company I worked for." (linkedin_post_002)
  "Last September I was at a trade fair in Mumbai." (linkedin_post_004)

b) A hard statistic in the first sentence
  "In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns came from
  customers who mentioned the texture or feel of the product as the
  reason." (linkedin_post_003)

c) A direct claim about the reader's own product
  "The niacinamide serum you're using probably has the ingredient
  listed at 5% or 10% on the label. That number is almost certainly
  meaningless without knowing three other things about the
  formulation." (linkedin_post_001)

d) A real customer question
  "A customer wrote to me last month asking whether she could use our
  serum with a Vitamin C product she'd been using for years."
  (newsletter_003)

e) An admission of hesitation or care
  "I've been avoiding writing this one because Vitamin C in skincare is
  genuinely complicated..." (newsletter_004)
  "I almost didn't write this email, because most founder retrospective
  posts I've read are structured as celebration..." (newsletter_011)
  "I want to be careful writing this one because I've seen this
  conversation go badly in both directions." (newsletter_009)

f) A blunt thesis up front
  "I want to start with something direct: this list is not going to be
  a place where I sell you things." (newsletter_001)
  "The shortest version of this email: fragrance is the ingredient
  category with the highest rate of contact sensitisation in
  cosmetics..." (newsletter_005)
  "Reapplying sunscreen is not optional. I know this isn't new
  information." (newsletter_006)


3. SENTENCE LENGTH AND RHYTHM
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Her default is long, clause-heavy explanatory sentences. She breaks
them up with short, flat declaratives, usually in runs of two or three.
The short sentences carry the verdict.

Short-sentence runs:
  "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is
  also not helpful." (linkedin_post_001)
  "I asked about the pH of the finished formula. The answer was 3.2."
  (linkedin_post_002)
  "Nobody else in the room asked about it. The formulation passed
  review. I left the company 7 months later." (linkedin_post_002)
  "The actives were intact. The formulation was working as designed. It
  was designed for the wrong context." (linkedin_post_003)
  "The ingredient is present. The formulation conditions for efficacy
  are not." (newsletter_010)
  "The sensitisation is permanent." (newsletter_005)

Signature construction: "X is [true/legal]. It is also [Y]."
  "This is true. It is also almost entirely useless information for
  evaluating whether a specific product does anything." (newsletter_002)
  "These are not unusual requirements. They are also not standard
  practice for most brands..." (linkedin_post_001)

Correction by contrast: "This is not A. This is B."
  "This is not a rounding issue. This is the difference between
  meaningful protection and almost none." (newsletter_006)
  "The base isn't decoration around the active. It determines whether
  the active arrives." (newsletter_002)

"Not wrong, but incomplete"
  "The standard advice to 'leave a few minutes between products' is not
  wrong but it's incomplete." (newsletter_003)
  "...most explainer content on this topic stops exactly one step
  before the part that matters." (newsletter_002)

Paragraphs run 3 to 7 sentences. She uses no bullets, headers, or bold
in the body text. When she lists items, she writes them into the
sentence.


4. HOW SHE USES DATA AND EVIDENCE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
a) Exact numbers, thresholds, and comparisons. She never writes
   "significant" without a figure nearby.
  "The active we were discussing has known degradation behaviour at pH
  below 3.8." [finished formula: pH 3.2] (linkedin_post_002)
  "71% were from customers in cities with average annual humidity above
  70%. Mumbai, Chennai, Kochi, Kolkata." (linkedin_post_003)
  "Returns from humid cities dropped to 8% in the following quarter.
  The active ingredient concentrations stayed identical."
  (linkedin_post_003)
  "our repeat purchase rate at 12 months is 67%. In a market where most
  DTC skincare brands report repeat rates between 20-40%..."
  (newsletter_011)
  "At half the tested amount, an SPF 30 product delivers protection
  closer to SPF 5-6." (newsletter_006)

b) Minimum-effective-dose thinking
  "For niacinamide, the research supporting sebum regulation starts at
  around 2% and gets more pronounced at 4-5%." (newsletter_002)

c) Vague vs. specific, side by side
  "'Contains niacinamide' tells you almost nothing. 'Contains
  niacinamide at 4%, pH 5.5-6.0, in a water-based base with documented
  six-month stability' tells you something." (newsletter_002)

d) She names exact compounds and does not stop at the category
  "palmitoyl pentapeptide-4 (Matrixyl), acetyl hexapeptide-3
  (Argireline)" (newsletter_010)
  "ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbyl phosphate, magnesium ascorbyl
  phosphate, ascorbyl glucoside, ethyl ascorbic acid" (newsletter_004)

e) She grades the evidence and does not flatten it
  "The in-vitro results are consistent. The clinical results are more
  variable..." (newsletter_010)
  "That doesn't mean it doesn't work. It means the evidence base is
  different..." (newsletter_004)
  "The key word is 'can.'" (newsletter_007)

f) She states what her own data cannot show
  "I don't know how to separate 'the formulation works' from 'the
  communication builds trust' in that number, and probably both are
  contributing. But I'll take it." (newsletter_011)
  "It is not the most flattering version of the information because
  some of our testing is early stage." (linkedin_post_004)

g) She attaches a cost to her own rigour
  "...a stability testing protocol that adds to our timelines and costs
  more than the industry standard approach." (linkedin_post_002)


5. THE "WHAT I'M NOT SAYING" MOVE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
Almost every piece has one. She narrows her claim before a critic can,
and she concedes the other side's valid points.

  "I want to be precise about what I'm not saying here. I'm not saying
  the people in that room were incompetent or malicious. I'm saying the
  system had a gap, the gap was not unusual..." (linkedin_post_002)
  "I am not making a case that niacinamide doesn't work."
  (linkedin_post_001)
  "I'm not trying to make you distrust every product on your shelf. I'm
  trying to explain what questions to ask..." (newsletter_002)
  "I'm not saying natural ingredients are better or worse than
  synthetic ones. That binary is one of the most unproductive framings
  in skincare." (newsletter_008)
  "I'm not telling you to abandon clean beauty standards if they matter
  to you. I'm saying that 'clean' is a marketing category, not a
  scientific one..." (newsletter_009)

Fair concessions:
  "These are real and legitimate commercial considerations."
  [on fragrance] (newsletter_005)
  "What clean beauty gets right: it pushed a meaningful number of brands
  to examine their ingredient lists..." (newsletter_009)

Honesty that works against her own position:
  "I don't use parabens in our formulations... But I don't avoid them
  because I believe they're dangerous at cosmetic concentrations,
  because the evidence doesn't support that." (newsletter_009)


6. HOW SHE CLOSES
---------------------------------------------------------------------
She ends on an action the reader can take, usually "ask the brand," and
often frames a non-answer as information in itself. There are no sales
calls to action.

  "If the answer is that they don't have that documentation, that's
  useful information." (linkedin_post_001)
  "If the answer is vague or doesn't arrive, that is also useful
  information." (linkedin_post_004)
  "If you email any skincare brand asking for the pH of their active
  product and they can't answer, that tells you something useful about
  how closely the formulation chemistry is being managed."
  (newsletter_003)
  "Strip back the routine before building it up." (newsletter_007)
  "When we get it right, I'll tell you exactly what we did and why."
  (newsletter_004)
  "Thank you for reading these emails. I mean that specifically, not as
  a sign-off." (newsletter_011)


7. WHAT SHE NEVER SAYS OR DOES
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- No exclamation marks, emojis, or hashtags anywhere in the 15 pieces.
- No hype words such as "game-changer," "miracle," "holy grail," or
  "glow," and no "we're thrilled/excited to announce."
- Industry buzzwords appear only inside quotation marks, and only so she
  can examine them: "Clinically tested," "natural," "clean," "barrier
  repair," "multi-peptide complex," "contains 7 peptides," and
  "skin-loving."
- She never names a competitor brand and never attributes bad motives.
  "Some of it is carelessness. Some of it is commercial pressure. Most
  of it is that the gap between formulation science and marketing
  communication is wide..." (linkedin_post_002)
- She claims no medical authority.
  "I'm not a dermatologist. I don't have a medical degree."
  (newsletter_001)
- She makes no claim ahead of her data.
  "We don't currently market any of our products primarily on barrier
  repair claims. When we have the formulation data to support that
  claim specifically, we will." (newsletter_007)
- She never pitches. She disclaims commercial interest explicitly.
  "I'm not selling a sunscreen. I want to be clear about that. This is
  not a setup for a product launch." (newsletter_006)
  "We don't currently sell a peptide product. I'm telling you this
  because I want the assessment to be straightforward - I don't have a
  commercial stake in how you read it." (newsletter_010)
  "You should ask for them. Not from us specifically - from any brand
  whose products you're spending significant money on."
  (linkedin_post_001)
  "I bring this up not because our reformulation is a selling point -
  it should have been right the first time." (linkedin_post_003)


8. HOW PERSONAL SHE GETS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
She is professionally personal: career history, decisions, mistakes,
and working frustrations. She never mentions family, relationships,
lifestyle, or her appearance. Her emotion is understated and named in
one word.

Career:
  "I spent two years working in pharmaceutical formulation before
  starting Skinstinct." (newsletter_001)
Mistakes, owned plainly:
  "...it took us embarrassingly long to make the connection."
  (newsletter_011)
  "The lesson is that having a pharmaceutical background doesn't make
  you immune to skipping the validation step for the specific market
  you're actually serving." (newsletter_011)
Understated emotion:
  "Skinstinct came out of the specific discomfort of sitting in that
  room..." (linkedin_post_002)
  "I find it genuinely interesting to write about." (newsletter_001)
Deflating her own story:
  "That is the entirety of the founding rationale. It is not a
  particularly inspiring story. It is a documentation gap and a
  decision to do something about it." (linkedin_post_002)
Candour about work that is not finished:
  "I have been trying to formulate one that I'm actually satisfied with
  for about 14 months." (newsletter_004)
  "One has been in development for over a year, which is longer than I
  planned and shorter than it apparently needed." (newsletter_011)


9. VOCABULARY AND MECHANICS
---------------------------------------------------------------------
- British spelling: oxidise, sensitisation, colour, moisturiser,
  standardised, oestrogenic, behaviour, categorisation.
- She uses technical terms and explains them in context: CoA, INCI,
  stratum corneum, transepidermal water loss, vasodilation.
- Recurring words: genuinely, specific/specifically, actually, honest,
  documentation, gap, useful, meaningful, evidence.
- She writes a spaced hyphen " - " as her dash.
- Numbers and percentages appear as numerals (23%, pH 3.2, 7 months,
  14 months).
- India is her default context, used concretely: "Mumbai in July,"
  "Chennai in August," "a Mumbai summer," "at our UV index levels."
- She uses "I" for judgement and decisions and "we" for the company's
  practice.


10. FORMAT BY CHANNEL
---------------------------------------------------------------------
LinkedIn post
- No greeting and no sign-off. Pure prose paragraphs, roughly 400-550
  words.
- Opens with a scene, a statistic, or a claim about the reader's own
  product.
- Ends with advice to "ask your brand/manufacturer," or a plain
  statement of what Skinstinct does.

Email newsletter
- Salutation is always just "Hi," with no name. Sign-off is always just
  "Meera." There is no P.S.
- Subject lines are plain, sometimes candid, and often use a dash,
  a parenthetical, or two short sentences:
    What you're actually signed up for
    What an "active" actually does (and why most explanations stop too
      early)
    pH - the question nobody puts on the label
    The Vitamin C email (I've been putting this off)
    Why there's no fragrance in anything we make
    The one thing I want you to stop doing with SPF
    "Natural" in a formulation lab means something very specific. It's
      not what the label implies.
    Clean beauty gets some things right. The rest is making things
      harder.
    Peptides: what the research actually says
    18 months of Skinstinct. Some data, some honesty.


11. TYPICAL PIECE STRUCTURE
---------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Concrete opening (scene, statistic, customer question, or admission)
2. The common or standard explanation, and why it stops too early
3. The mechanism, with exact numbers and thresholds
4. What she is NOT saying, plus a fair concession
5. What she IS saying, in one short, flat sentence
6. A practical question the reader can ask any brand
7. Optional: what Skinstinct does, stated plainly, with any gaps or
   costs admitted and no pitch


12. QUICK CHECKLIST FOR WRITING AS MEERA
---------------------------------------------------------------------
DO
[ ] Open on something specific and real, not a hook question
[ ] Put a number next to every claim of scale
[ ] Name the exact ingredient, derivative, pH, or percentage
[ ] Follow long explanations with a 2-3 sentence flat verdict
[ ] Say what you're not saying
[ ] Admit what the data can't show, or what you got wrong
[ ] End with a question the reader can put to any brand
[ ] Use British spelling and India-specific context

DON'T
[ ] Use exclamation marks, emojis, hashtags, or hype adjectives
[ ] Use buzzwords except in quotation marks, under examination
[ ] Name or attack competitors, or assume bad intent
[ ] Imply medical authority or make claims ahead of the data
[ ] Pitch products, add sales calls to action, or offer discounts
[ ] Share personal-life details beyond her work

Off-voice (invented, for contrast):
  "Our clinically proven serum is a total game-changer for glowing,
  healthy skin! Shop now."
On-voice (real, newsletter_002):
  "We publish that information for our products because it exists and
  because I think you should have it."
