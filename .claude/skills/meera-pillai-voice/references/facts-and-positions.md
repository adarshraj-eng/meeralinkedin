## Science positions she has stated
- Niacinamide: water-soluble, relatively stable; converts to niacin at low pH (more aggressively below pH 4), causing vasodilation/flushing. Sebum-regulation evidence from ~2%, stronger at 4-5%, some benefits at 10%. Evidence for sebum regulation, barrier support, hyperpigmentation is solid.
- pH windows: ascorbic acid 2.5-3.5; AHAs work at 3-4, largely inert above 4.5.
- Layering with Vitamin C: Vitamin C first (ideally pH below 3.5), wait 5-10 minutes, then Skinstinct serum.
- Vitamin C: L-ascorbic acid has the strongest evidence, least stable; oxidises to orange-yellow. Derivatives (ethyl ascorbic acid, sodium ascorbyl phosphate etc.) more stable, thinner evidence; some brands market derivatives against L-AA data.
- Retinol: needs to reach the dermis; vehicle matters enormously.
- Stratum corneum lipids: ~50% ceramides, 25% cholesterol, 15% free fatty acids.
- Ceramides listed after preservatives in INCI are at trace levels.
- Peptides: mechanism real; Matrixyl and Argireline have the strongest research; in-vitro consistent, clinical variable; generally need delivery help; "7 peptides at sub-therapeutic levels" is not better than 2 at effective levels.
- Fragrance: highest contact-sensitisation category in cosmetics; "parfum" can hide many compounds; sensitisation develops over repeated exposure and is permanent. General-population prevalence of clinically relevant fragrance contact allergy is **1.9% (95% CI 1.5-2.5)**, rising to **2.6% (CI 2.1-3.2)** for fragrance mix I specifically; roughly twice as common in women as in men; higher with existing skin conditions. ⚠ These are European figures - see verified references.
- SPF: rated at 2mg/cm²; people apply 0.5-1mg/cm²; SPF in moisturiser is not a substitute for dedicated sunscreen; apply last, reapply every two hours of continuous outdoor exposure. The SPF-to-quantity relationship is exponential, not linear: at **1mg/cm² (half dose) effective SPF falls to roughly the square root** of the labelled figure, and at **0.5mg/cm² to roughly the fourth root**. So a labelled SPF 30 applied at half dose behaves closer to 5-6, and at quarter dose closer to 2-3.
- Parabens: concern originated from oestrogenic activity at concentrations far above cosmetic use; evidence of harm at cosmetic levels essentially absent; some substitutes less studied and more sensitising.
- "Natural", "clean", and "clinically tested": marketing categories with **no legal definition or certification standard** in India. They are not, however, unregulated - an unsubstantiated claim is actionable as misleading under the Cosmetics Rules 2020 and the ASCI code. The precise position is "undefined, but not unpoliced": nobody certifies what "natural" means, yet a brand that cannot evidence the claim is in breach. Natural vs synthetic remains an unproductive binary.

## Verified references (checked 24 September 2026)

All five previously flagged claims have been researched. Use the corrected wording below rather than the original phrasing. Three were wrong or imprecise; two were sound.

### 1. EU fragrance allergens - CORRECTED, and now time-sensitive
The familiar "26 allergens" figure is **out of date**. Commission Regulation (EU) 2023/1545 added **56 new fragrance allergens** to Annex III of Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009, taking the disclosable list to **about 82**. It entered into force 16 August 2023.

Both deadlines matter, and the first has already passed:
- **31 July 2026** - non-compliant products could no longer be *placed on* the EU market. This date is behind us.
- **31 July 2028** - non-compliant stock already in the supply chain can still be *made available* to consumers.

Correct usage: "the EU's disclosable fragrance allergen list, expanded from 26 to around 82 in 2023". Never write "the 26 EU allergens" as current fact. Note also that this is EU law and does not apply in India, which is usually the more interesting point for her audience.

### 2. The Liverpool SPF study - CORRECTED on year and on subject
There is no 2022 University of Liverpool study on SPF *application amount*. The real work is Hamill's group at Liverpool on **coverage**, which is a different claim:
- **2019, PLOS ONE** (Lourenco, Shaw, Pratt, Duffy, Czanner, Zheng, McCormick, Hamill): participants missed **14.0%** of the eyelid region using sunscreen, versus **20.9%** using an SPF moisturiser, and were unaware of the gap afterwards.
- **2017**: earlier UV-imaging work from the same group showing facial areas at high skin-cancer risk are disproportionately missed.

This is actually a stronger fact for her SPF-in-moisturiser position than the one she cited. Cite it as coverage, not dose, and use 2019.

### 3. "Clinically tested" / "natural" in India - CORRECTED, too strong as written
"Not regulated" overstates it. There is **no legal definition or certification standard** for either term in Indian cosmetics law, but unsubstantiated claims **are** actionable as misleading under the **Cosmetics Rules 2020** (labels must not be false or misleading) and the **ASCI code**. ASCI's 2023-24 annual report found 98% of scrutinised beauty advertisements required modification.

Correct framing: "undefined, but not unpoliced" - nobody certifies what "natural" means, yet a brand that cannot evidence the claim is in breach. This is more precise than the original and makes the same point better.

### 4. SPF 30 at half dose ≈ 5-6 - CONFIRMED
Supported by **Faurschou & Wulf, British Journal of Dermatology, 2007**, the first in vivo confirmation that SPF relates to applied quantity exponentially rather than linearly. At 1mg/cm² effective SPF falls to the square root of the label (√30 ≈ 5.5); at 0.5mg/cm² to the fourth root (30^0.25 ≈ 2.3).

One honest caveat worth stating if the number carries weight in a piece: the study tested **SPF 4 sunscreen on 20 volunteers**, so applying the model up to SPF 30 is an extrapolation. "Follows roughly the square root at half the recommended amount" is defensible; "is exactly SPF 5.5" is not.

### 5. 1-3% sensitisation rate - CONFIRMED, and can be made more precise
From the **EDEN Fragrance Study** (five European countries - Germany, Italy, Netherlands, Portugal, Sweden; 12,377 interviewed, 3,119 patch tested): clinically relevant fragrance contact allergy prevalence **1.9% (95% CI 1.5-2.5)**, and **2.6% (CI 2.1-3.2)** for fragrance mix I. Across the wider literature the range is 0.7-2.6%. Roughly twice as prevalent in women as in men.

Prefer the specific figure with its confidence interval over the "1-3%" range. **These are European general-population data**; there is no equivalent Indian dataset here, and saying so is more on-voice than quietly generalising it.
