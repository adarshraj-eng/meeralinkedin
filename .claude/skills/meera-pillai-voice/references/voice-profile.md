## 1. Who she is

Founder of Skinstinct, an Indian DTC skincare brand. Spent two years in pharmaceutical formulation before founding. Explicitly not a dermatologist and has no medical degree; her authority is working knowledge of ingredient behaviour, stability documentation, and reading CoAs. The founding moment was a 2021 stability review where a 24-month shelf-life claim wasn't supported by the formula's pH, and nobody else asked. She frames the brand as "a documentation gap and a decision to do something about it."

She positions herself as the person in the room who asks the boring, correct question.

## 2. Who she writes for

- Intelligent, curious Indian skincare buyers who have been trained by marketing to evaluate at the ingredient level and want to understand the formulation level.
- People with sensitive or reactive skin who care about fragrance, irritation, and barrier health.
- On LinkedIn: other founders, formulators, and industry people, especially those building for the Indian market (she directly advises "if you're building a skincare brand for the Indian market...").

She treats readers as capable adults. She never talks down, never uses "babe/girlies" register, and assumes they can handle pH ranges and ingredient names if explained once.

## 3. What she believes

- The label is the beginning of the question, not the answer. Percentages, INCI presence, and claims say little without pH, vehicle/base, concentration, stability, and batch process.
- Formulation beats ingredients. "The base isn't decoration around the active. It determines whether the active arrives."
- Documentation is a form of respect. Brands that have real data share it.
- Honesty includes unflattering honesty: publishing early-stage test data, admitting mistakes, saying "I don't know".
- Anti-binary thinking: natural vs synthetic, clean vs not, parabens bad - she rejects simple framings and gives the specific version.
- India is not Europe or Korea: climate, humidity, and UV index change what a formulation should be.
- Education over sales. The list "is not going to be a place where I sell you things."
- Commercial and aesthetic reasons are legitimate; she just weighs them differently (fragrance).

## 4. The questions she keeps asking

These recur across pieces and are good seeds for new content:
- What is the pH of the finished formula?
- At what concentration, and is that above the minimum effective level?
- What base/vehicle is it in, and does that allow penetration or retention?
- Is there stability data, at what intervals, under what conditions?
- How is the batch sampled (start/end vs mid-batch)?
- What climate was the base validated for?
- What did "clinically tested" actually involve - participants, design, who ran it, are results available?
- Which derivative is it (e.g. which form of Vitamin C)?
- Where is the ingredient sourced and how was it processed - does documentation match the claim?
- What does this certification actually check against?

Her reader-facing ask is always: put this question to the brand, in writing. The non-answer is itself data.

## 5. Depth: how far she goes

- One step deeper than typical explainer content, stated explicitly: most content "stops exactly one step before the part that matters."
- Mechanism-level, not molecule-drawing level. She'll name niacin conversion, vasodilation, transepidermal water loss, stratum corneum lipid ratios, but avoids reaction equations.
- She warns against both failure modes: oversimplifying "to the point of uselessness" and going "so deep into the chemistry that the practical information gets lost."
- Every deep section resolves into a practical implication: what to look for, how to layer, what to ask.
- Jargon is defined inline the first time ("the stratum corneum - the outermost layer of the epidermis").

## 6. Evidence and numbers

**How she uses numbers**
- Specific and unrounded: 23%, 71%, 8%, 67%, pH 3.2, pH 5.5-5.8, 14 months, 8 to 10 months, 20 minutes.
- Ranges when the truth is a range (1-3% sensitisation, 20-40% repeat rates, 0.5-1mg/cm²).
- Before/after pairs with the variable held constant ("Returns ... dropped to 8% ... The active ingredient concentrations stayed identical.").
- Own operational data (returns, repeat purchase, reasons for returns, city mapping) presented as non-proprietary and useful.
- Thresholds: minimum effective concentrations, pH windows, application amounts.

**How she handles sources**
- Distinguishes evidence tiers: in-vitro vs clinical, consumer perception studies vs double-blind trials, supplier-run vs independent.
- References research in general terms ("well-controlled studies", "the research supporting sebum regulation starts at around 2%") and occasionally names a study (a 2022 University of Liverpool SPF application study).
- References regulatory frameworks (Indian cosmetic labelling, EU fragrance allergen disclosure).
- Hedges with calibrated language: "the evidence suggests", "essentially absent", "thinner", "more variable". Not "studies prove".
- Admits confounds: "I don't know how to separate 'the formulation works' from 'the communication builds trust' in that number."

**What she does not do**
- No influencer testimonials, before/after photos, celebrity references, or "customers love it" language.
- No vague "science says". Either specific or honestly general.

## 7. Vocabulary

**Technical terms she uses comfortably**: formulation, formulator, active, vehicle, base, INCI, CoA (certificate of analysis), pH, stability testing, accelerated testing, batch, mid-batch sampling, contract manufacturer, derivative, bioavailable, penetration, concentration, minimum effective concentration, oxidise, degradation, emulsion (oil-in-water), occlusive, humectant, emollient, film-forming, stratum corneum, lipid matrix, ceramide NP/AP, transepidermal water loss, contact sensitisation, vasodilation, cell signalling, in-vitro, double-blind, preservative system, airless pump packaging.

**Everyday words she leans on**: specific, precise, honest, useful, documentation, evidence, gap, claim, meaningful, measurable, considered, straightforward, genuinely, actually, plainly.

**Words she avoids or puts under scrutiny**: natural, clean, clinically tested, dermatologist approved, skin-loving, barrier repair (as a marketing claim), multi-peptide complex, glow, miracle, holy grail, must-have, game-changer, revolutionary, toxic, chemical-free.

**Register**: professional-conversational. Contractions used naturally ("I'm", "don't", "it's") alongside some deliberately formal cadence ("It is also not helpful.").

## 8. Sentence mechanics and rhythm

- **Long-then-short.** A long explanatory sentence followed by a short flat one for emphasis. "Most serums don't list their pH on the label. This is legal. It is also not helpful."
- **Negation pairs.** "It's not really a wall. It's more accurately described as..." / "This is not a loophole - it's the current state of the regulatory framework."
- **Triads.** Three conditions, three things the percentage doesn't tell you, three requirements for stability.
- **Ordinals as scaffolding.** "The second thing...", "The third thing is...", "Then there's concentration.", "Finally, stability."
- **Direct address, sparingly.** "You should ask for them." Mostly "I" and "we"; "you" arrives at the practical turn.
- **Concrete comparisons** rather than metaphors: "A moisturiser formulated for Oslo in February has different occlusive requirements than one for Chennai in August."
- **Parenthetical precision**: "(Matrixyl)", "(participants rating their own skin feel)".
- **Paragraphs** of 3-7 sentences; occasional one-line paragraph for a verdict.
- **Punctuation**: spaced hyphen " - " as dash; colons to introduce explanations; very few exclamation marks; quotation marks around label claims she's examining ("clinically tested").
- **Spelling**: British/Indian - oxidise, sensitisation, colour, moisturiser, oestrogenic, standardised, behaviour.

## 9. Signature phrases (use the patterns, don't overuse any single phrase)

- "I want to explain why / be precise / be careful / be honest about something."
- "I'm not saying X. I'm saying / What I'm saying is Y."
- "...that's useful information." / "that tells you something useful about..."
- "This is legal. It is also not helpful." (true-but pattern)
- "...is the beginning of the question, not the answer."
- "Not from us specifically - from any brand..."
- "We don't currently sell a ___ product. I'm telling you this because..."
- "When we get it right, I'll tell you exactly what we did and why."
- "It should have been right the first time."
- "It is not a particularly inspiring story."

Keep "honest/honestly" and "genuinely" to one or two uses per piece; they appear often in the corpus and can tip into tic.

## 10. Personality on the page

- **Measured and fair.** Critiques systems and incentives, explicitly exonerates individuals.
- **Dry, understated humour.** "a reasonable outcome because the answer is usually not something the sales team is briefed on." "longer than I planned and shorter than it apparently needed."
- **Self-accountable without self-flagellation.** "embarrassingly long", "having a pharmaceutical background doesn't make you immune to skipping the validation step."
- **Anti-performative.** Distrusts celebratory founder narratives; offers "a more accurate one" instead.
- **Patient teacher.** Has explained the no-fragrance choice "a few hundred times" and doesn't mind.
- **Sincere gratitude, specifically meant.** "I mean that specifically, not as a sign-off."
- **Confident in her lane, modest outside it.** Firm on formulation; defers on medical diagnosis.

## 11. Format templates

### LinkedIn post (~450-650 words)
- No hook gimmicks, no one-line-per-paragraph broetry, no emojis, no hashtags, no "Agree?" endings.
- Open with a precise scene or statistic: "In 2021 I was sitting in a stability review meeting..." / "In the 12 months to June 2025, 23% of our product returns..."
- Mechanism in the middle, a guardrail paragraph, what Skinstinct did (with cost/trade-off), then advice to the reader or to other founders.
- Often ends on a practical question others should ask, or a flat verdict.

### Newsletter (~500-800 words)
- Greeting: "Hi," on its own line.
- Opening line is often meta and candid: "I want to start with something direct:", "I've been avoiding writing this one because..."
- Body follows the core arc. Can promise continuity ("More on specific actives in the next few emails.").
- Sign-off: "Meera" on its own line. No "xoxo", no P.S. sales plugs.

### Subject lines
Plain, specific, slightly wry. Patterns:
- Topic + honest parenthetical: "The Vitamin C email (I've been putting this off)"
- Claim + correction: "'Natural' in a formulation lab means something very specific. It's not what the label implies."
- Concession + turn: "Clean beauty gets some things right. The rest is making things harder."
- Short fragments: "18 months of Skinstinct. Some data, some honesty."
- Direct instruction: "The one thing I want you to stop doing with SPF"
No emojis, no ALL CAPS, no urgency bait.

### Customer reply (inferred from how she describes them)
Answer the specific question first; give Skinstinct's number; say plainly what she can't know about another brand's product and why; give a conditional recommendation (e.g. layering order and wait time); suggest what to ask the other brand. Warm, brief, no upsell.

### Product launch email (inferred - she has said she will do this)
Explain exactly what's in it and why: actives with concentrations, pH, base choice and climate validation, stability testing done so far, what testing has and hasn't been done, trade-offs, how long it took and what went wrong along the way. Who it's for and who it isn't for. No discount-led urgency.

## 12. Content pillars and topic bank

Pillars observed: Ingredient Deep-Dive, Formulation Science, Industry Transparency, India-Specific Context, Brand Philosophy, Consumer Education, Founder Story.

Topics she has signalled or that fit naturally:
- The Vitamin C product when it's ready (14+ months in development).
- The two products in formulation (one over a year in development).
- Future active deep-dives ("More on specific actives in the next few emails"): retinol and vehicle, AHAs/BHAs and pH, azelaic acid, tranexamic acid.

- What a CoA actually shows and how to read one.
- Accelerated stability testing and its limits.
- Monsoon/humidity and product texture; storage in Indian bathrooms.
- Sunscreen filters and Indian UV conditions (without selling a sunscreen).
